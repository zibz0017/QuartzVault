# Shmuess 1
##  במדבר כז ז עם רש"י שם וספר זכרון על רש"י
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The Bnos Tzlafchad understood that they were entitled to inherit their father's land. They were able to see something that משה was not able to be. They would not have asked for the land unless they were certain that they were entitled to it.</span>

### Rebbi's Explanation
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The Bnos Tzlafchad were able to think of this because they had extra motivation. They didn't want their father's name to be erased. As such, they were able to realize that there was a way for them to inherit the land and so their father's name would not be erased.</span>
## Shmuess
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">We see the power of motivation. Obviously, the Bnos Tzlafchad were not on the same מדרגה as משה רבינו. However, because they had extra motivation to try and think of something, they were able to think of something that משה himself was unsure of.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Additionally, this is interesting because we usually say the negios are detrimental to arriving at the truth. We usually say that one should try and make cold, hard, fully intellectual calculations when deciding on an issue. Over here, it seems that the extra motivation helped then.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">It seems that a negiah is only one reason that someone may answer a shailah incorrectly. Another reason is that the person simply didn't care enough to research it as properly as they could have. As such, having extra motivation can actually help a person arrive at the correct answer.</span>

<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Rav Chaim Volozhin {<span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">I think. I need to come back to this later to confirm this</span>} said that sometimes, he would examine a shailah about an agunah and his learning would lead him to be מחמיר. No matter, how he looked at the sugya, despite his intense iyun, that seemed to be the halacha.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">However, once he became a rav and these shailos weren't just theoretical, he had to פסקען for actual people, he felt the responsibility for these people to try and help them. By pushing himself to his maximum, he was able to come up with heteirim for these people.</span>
## Shmuess
*Note: This wasn't actually a shmuess.*
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Once again, we see the power of motivation. Even though of course he was already putting in tremendous effort simply to understand the sugya (putting aside the fact that even though he wasn't directly responsible, he probably wanted to try and find a התר for the woman anyway), once the shailos became real, he was able to come up with heteirim.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Once again, we also see the idea that even though this can be a negiah to make a mistake, this was not the case. This was extra fuel and motivation and helped him arrive at the truth rather than blind him to it.</span>
# Shmuess 3
## ויקרא רבה יט ו (טו כה)
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Nebuchadnezzar imprisoned King Yechonya and was never going to let him out of prison, as he never let any prisoner out.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Yechonya was the last living descendant of Dovid Hamelech and he didn't have any children at that point. If he would die, this would break the lineage of Dovid Hamelech.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The rabanan of the time got together to try and think of a plan to somehow be able to keep Dovid Hamelech's lineage alive. They galvanized themselves by saying "is Dovid Hamelch's lineage going to break in our times?"</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">In the end, they were able to get permission for Yechonya's wife to be able to go to into the prison and be with Yechonya and was able to leave. This continued the chain of descendants from Dovid Hamelech.</span>
## בראשית רבה סא ו (כה ה)
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Avraham had a difficult dilemma. He needed to give Yitchak the brachos and he couldn't give Yishmael. However, if he would give Yitzchak the brachos then he would need to give Yishmael the brachos as well. And the only way he could avoid giving Yishmael the brachos would be by not giving the brachos to Yitzchak either.</spaan>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This is comparable to a king who has a field and hires someone to take care of it. However, there are two trees that are intertwined: a tree with סַם חַיִּים and another with סַם הַמָּוֶת. There is no way to water one without watering the other. The sharecropper was in a difficult situation. He needed to water the סַם חַיִּים tree but he couldn't water the סַם הַמָּוֶת. He decided that this was not his field to work things out, it was the king's. As just a caretaker, he couldn't actively go and water the סַם הַמָּוֶת tree. As such, he decided to be שב ואל תעשה and not water either and allow the king to deal with the problem.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Avraham similarly did not give Yitzchak a bracha. He decided to be שב ואל תעשה and allow Hashem to deal with it (which Hashem did, by giving Yitzchak the brachos himself. Clearly Hashem did not have the same constraints and He was allowed to give Yitzchak a bracha without giving Yishmael).</span>
## Shmuess
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Hashem guaranteed that Dovid Hamelech's descendants would never be wiped out. Obviously, the rabanan were not literally concerned that Dovid Hamelech's lineage would cease. They were obviously not כופרים. It was simply a tool that they used to galvanize themselves. They viewed the situation as if it was in their hands to decide the fate of what happens.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">As such, on the one hand, we see how hard we need to push to try and affect the outcome of a situation in a positive manner, even though we know that it is totally in the hands of Hashem and not up to us. If Hashem wants the thing to happen, Hashem will make it happen, and if not, then it won't. Yet we can't view it that way. We have to view it like it is up to us, and it will only happen if we make it happen.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">At the same time, we also need to be cognizant of doing the right thing and realizing that it is not up to us. Avraham knew that it would be wrong for him to give the brachos to Yitzchak and not give them to Yishmael as well. As such, he decided against giving them. The entire future of כלל ישראל seemingly rested on this decision. We are talking about the brachos. This is immeasurably important. Why for something as important as this, wouldn't it seem prudent to give the brachos and ignore the Yishmael?</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">We see that we also need to maintain the mentality that it is not our world. Our job is simply to do the right thing and not do the wrong thing. If it is wrong to give Yitzchak the brachos and not Yishmael, then Avraham can't do it. What will be with the future of כלל ישראל? That is for Hashem to figure out.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">So on the one hand, we have to view the situation as if we are the only ones who can affect the outcome. On the other, we have to realize that our job is simply to do the right thing and Hashem can figure things out without us. If whatever it is that we are trying to do is supposed to happen, it will happen without us, and if it isn't supposed to happen, it won't happen, regardless of how hard we try.</span>
# Shmuess 4
## בראשית רבה פ ד (לד א)
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Because Yaakov did not marry Dina off to Esav (which could have been a catalyst for him to do תשובה), Dina ended up marrying Iyov (who was not Jewish) and was attacked by Shechem.</span>

## בראשית רבה סז ד (כז לד)
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Yaakov caused Esav tremendous pain by taking the brachos. Because of that, Haman, the descendant of Esav, caused Yaakov's descendants tremendous pain (they were terrified they would be destroyed) in the story of Purim.</span>
### Both Medrashim Explained by Rebbi
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Obviously, Yaakov was not supposed to marry Dina off to Esav and he was supposed to take the brachos. The medrash means that Yaakov didn't feel enough emotion about not doing, or doing, these actions. He should have felt more for Esav.</span>
## Shmuess
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">We see how far feeling someone else's pain goes. Even when nothing good comes out of feeling someone else's pain, it is still important to feel their pain, regardless. Obviously Yaakov felt Esav's pain to some degree. Despite this, Yaakov was punished rather severely. We see that this is important.</span>
