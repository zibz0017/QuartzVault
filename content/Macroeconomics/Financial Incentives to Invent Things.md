# The Problem
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">We want people to try to invent things people need. As such, we want to incentivize them to do so.[^1]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">However, for some things, the main labor is inventing it, not producing it. An example of this is pharmaceuticals. It can take $1 billion to patent a drug, but $.50 to produce a pill.[^1]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">As such, the group that invented the drug would have no way to recoup their investment, and so they may have little incentive to try and invent it.[^1]</span>
# Solutions
## Patents
### Patents: Basic Idea
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;"> A patent is basically a temporary monopoly on a certain product. This means that only a certain group or entity is allowed to produce and sell a certain product.[^1]</span>
### Pros
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The free market decides what people patent. You can patent a bicycle with square wheels, but if nobody wants it, you won't make any money. this encourages people to try an patent things that people want.[^1]</span>
### Cons
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This can keep the availability for the product low. Only one individual producer can produce the good. This will also naturally keep the price high.[^1]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This may limit others who are able to utilize the idea from being able to do so.[^1]</span>
### Points to Consider
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">How long should a patent last?[^1]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">What can be patented? Can Amazon's One-click Buy be patented?[^1]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">How unique does a new idea need to be to not be considered an infringement of the patent?[^1]</span>
## Prizes
### Prizes: Basic Idea
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Prizes can be given to encourage people for inventing or discovering things.[^1]</span>
### Pros
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Prizes can be particularly useful because some things can have tremendous benefits (like fundamental scientific discoveries) but are not able to be monetized.[^1]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Prizes can be given by the government or by private citizens/groups.[^1]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Prizes can be offered to anyone who thinks of a solution to a problem, and they can go about it any way they want to. They don't have to have any official degree or anything like that.[^1]</span>
### Cons
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">How do you decide what deserves to get a prize? This isn't decided by consumer discretion.[^1]</span>
## Subsidies
### Subsidies: Basic Idea
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Money given to institutions that perform research, like colleges.[^1]</span>
### Pros
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Like prizes, this is useful for discovering things that can't be monetized.[^1]</span>
### Cons
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">How do you decide which institutions get subsidies? This isn't decided by consumer discretion.[^1]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">What forces the institutions to work on the things that people want? They may just work on things that they find interesting.[^1]</span>
