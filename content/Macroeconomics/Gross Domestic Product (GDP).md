# Basic Definition
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The market value of all finished goods and services produced within a country, in a year.[^1]</span>
# Explanation
## Market Value
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The item has to be bought and sold to have a market value.[^1]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">As such, charitable work, domestic work (like babysitting your child or mowing your lawn), free goods and services, and things without a price (like if a polar bear is born is a zoo) do not count toward GDP.[^1]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This does not mean that these things are not valuable and are not good. Being counted or not being counted toward GDP has nothing to do with that.[^1]</span>
4. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The reason why this is because it is difficult to calculate how much these things are worth.[^1] {<span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">I'm not sure how that applies to everything that is not bought or sold as many of these things have a relatively set price, such as babysitting, for example.</span>}</span>
## Finished Goods, Intermediate Goods, and Capital Goods
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Things bought by a final consumer are included in GDP.[^1]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Things bought by someone to be made into something else and sold are called "intermediate goods" and are not included in GDP.[^1]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Things bought by someone as a tool to create something that will be sold is called a "capital good" and is included in GDP.[^1]</span>
	<span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Examples:</span>
	1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">A person buys some eggs to make an omelet. The eggs are a finished good and are included in GDP.</span>
	 2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">A baker buys some eggs to make a cake to sell in his store. The eggs are intermediate goods and are not counted toward GDP. The cake is, though.</span>
	 3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">A farmer buys a tractor to help grow crops. This tractor is a capital good and is counted toward GDP, even thought the tractor is used by the farmer to grow crops that are sold.</span>
## Services
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Services are included in GDP.[^1]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A plumber comes to your house to fix some pipes and charges you for it. This is included in GDP.
## Within a Country
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Things which are imported from a country count toward the exporting country's GDP, not the importing country's.[^1]</span>
   {<span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">How do we evaluate a product which was partially assembled in different countries? Which country gets the GDP of that product?</span>}
## Produced Within a Year
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">If something was produced before a given year and then sold, it does not count toward the GDP of the year it was sold.[^1]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> An old house was sold. This does not count toward GDP of the year that it was sold in, as it was not produced within that year.
   {<span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">If the house was never sold before, then maybe it does count. Practically, you only know how much the house is worth from how much it was sold for, so until it is sold, it may not count toward GDP. I'm not sure about this point.</span>}
# Nominal GDP vs Real GDP, and GDP per Capita
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">GDP generally changes for three reasons:</span>
	1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">A country produces more stuff.[^2]</span>
	2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">A country produces better stuff.[^2] {<span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">Although if a country finds a way to produce the same thing for less money, that would seem to count against its GDP.</span>}</span>
	3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">[[Inflation]].[^2]</span>
## Nominal GDP
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Nominal GDP measures all three points.[^2]</span>
## Real GDP
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Real GDP measures the first two points.[^2]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Real GDP is usually the more important figure as it is used to see if a country actually produced more goods and services.[^2]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> Country A produces a lot more in one year than in the previous year, and Country B produces the same as it always does but has high levels of inflation. The nominal GDP of the two countries will be similar, but the Real GDP will not be. Using the real GDP, we can actually see that one of these countries is actually producing more and the other is not.
## GDP per Capita
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">It is important to measure the GDP per capita (per person) as opposed to just regular GDP as this gives a clearer picture of the production of a country.[^2]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A country's GDP stays stable over time. However, the population grows. This would indicate that the country is producing less per person, and that each person has less.
## Coming Together
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Combining what has been said, to get a measure of a country's level of production, one looks at the real GDP per capita.[^2]</span>
### Recession
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">When the real GDP per capita shrinks, that is usually a sign that we are in a recession.[^2]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> In 2009 (one year in The Great Recession) the real GDP per capita shrunk by 3.43%.[^3]
# Calculating GDP
## National Spending Approach
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Calculates GDP by calculating the amount of money spent.[^4]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">GDP is divided into 4 parts:</span>
	1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Consumption (things that everyday people buy, like groceries and plane tickets).[^4]</span> ^5e9b23
	2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Investment (things generally bought by businesses, like tractors and computers).[^4]</span> ^53841a
	3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Government spending (which can include things for the office work of government, like computers, or military spending, like tanks).[^4]</span>
	   Unemployment, social security, and other government programs are not included in GDP. This is because this is just a redistribution of money, not money being used to purchase a good or a service. When they are spent by the people who receive them, then they are included in GDP.[^4]
	4. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Net exports (meaning the amount we export minus the amount we import).[^4]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Generally, in the U.S. the breakdown is something like 63% for consumption, and government spending slightly more than investment. Net exports is about zero (meaning, we export and import about the same amount).[^4]</span>
4. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">When we refer to GDP, we generally refer to this approach of calculating the GDP.[^4]</span>
## Factor Income Approach
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Calculates GDP by calculating the amount of money earned.[^4]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">GDP is divided into:</span>
	1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Employee compensation (salaries and benefits).[^4]</span>
	2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Rent.[^4]</span>
	3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Interest.[^4]</span>
	4. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Profit.[^4]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Generally referred to as Gross Domestic Income (GDI).[^4]</span>
## Why We Use Both Methods
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">While theoretically GDP and GDI should be come out to exactly the same, because of some slight mistakes, they come out to be slightly different.[^4]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">As such, one or the other may have some indication toward the a future economic turn before the other. Therefore, it can be useful to look at both measures.[^4]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Another reason it is useful to look at both measures, particularly, the factors that go into the measures is that depending on which factors are increasing or decreasing, there are different economic tools that can be used to combat a negative turn.[^4]</span>

# Theories of Economic Growth
## The Solow Model

### Originator
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This Solow model of economic growth was theorized by Robert Solow, a Jewish economist who later won the Nobel Prize.[^20] </span>
### Factors of Growth
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Economic growth is based on the following three or four points:</span>
	1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Inventions and organization.[^21]</span>
	2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Labor, and the education and training of workers. (Can be considered two separate points or one point.)[^21]</span>
	3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Physical capital.[^21]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The more you have of these points, the greater the economic output is.[^21]</span>
### Investment
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">An economy needs to invest a certain percentage of the overall production to create more physical capital which can be used to further increase production.[^21]</span>
### Limiting Factors
#### Diminishing Returns
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">As there is with anything else in life, there are diminishing returns. This means that for every increase in physical capital and educated labor there is, there is an increase in production, but that increase in production gets smaller and smaller.[^22][^23]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A farmer has a tractor. He uses it to plow his fields. If he gets a second tractor, he may just keep it as a spare in case the first tractor breaks down. This leads to an increase in production (now the farmer can keep plowing his fields while he waits for his first tractor to be repaired whenever it breaks down) but it will not be as great of an increase in production as the farmer getting his first tractor (assuming that the first tractor isn't constantly breaking down).
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">I would think that perhaps there should be some slow growth which fairly quickly picks up speed as many things which can be produced are only useful in tandem with other things.</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A country may have a factory to produce steel, but it isn't so useful if there aren't any roads and trucks to bring it anywhere. Even if there are roads and trucks, there needs to be construction equipment to actually build something with it.[^18] ^2deee7
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">{<span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">Diminishing returns when it comes to labor is confusing because as labor increases, by definition, the population is increasing, which means that there is increased need for the labor. As such, returns shouldn't diminish much as labor increases.</span>}</span>
#### Depreciation
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Another point is that physical capital and educated labor depreciate over time. Physical capital starts to break down. People get older and eventually die. Replacing capital and labor takes time and resources. As such, some of the resources used for [[#Investment|investment]] need to be used for maintenance.[^24][^23]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The more physical capital and labor there is in an economy, the more depreciation there is. The increase is mostly at a constant rate.[^24][^23]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Obviously, things which are used less do break down slower, but some types of depreciation occur regardless (metal rusts, road get damaged by weather, etc.). And labor, assuming safe work conditions, depreciates at the same rate whether one is working or not.[^18]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> If you have 100 tons of iron and it rusts at a rate of 1% a year (meaning, 1% every year becomes unusable because of rust), you are going to have to replace 1 ton. If you have 1000 tons, it will also rust at approximately the same rate and so you will need to replace approximately 10 tons of it.
### The Steady-State
#### Reason for the Steady-State
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">To review, we [[#Investment|invest]] a certain percentage of what was produced in the economy back into the economy to help it grow.[^24]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The economy keeps growing the more we put into it, but it grows at a [[#Diminishing Returns|slower pace]], meaning, the same amount of capital (physical or human) adds less and less additional production.[^24]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Since we are investing a percentage of the total output, the amount invested follows the same trajectory as the total output, meaning, more capital means more investment, but less and less additional investment the more capital there already is.[^24]</span>
4. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This is different than [[#Depreciation|depreciation]]. As was said, depreciation follows a mostly constant trajectory.[^24]</span>
5. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">As such, at some point, maintenance "catches up" with investment, meaning, at some point adding more capital into an economy will not add any additional output. At this point, all investment is going toward maintenance and adding additional capital will not help as the additional amount invested from what is produced by this additional capital is less than the amount it costs to maintain that additional capital.[^24]</span>
6. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">At this point, the economy can not grow.[^24]</span>
7. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This is what is meant by the "steady-state". There is a point of equilibrium that the economy will grow to that point (because investing will lead to growth in production which leads to more investment at a greater rate than depreciation and maintenance) but not beyond that (because at that point, any new investment will not lead to more production than there is depreciation and maintenance of the invested capital).[^24]</span>
8. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">If the economy does invest more than that amount, slowly things will break faster than they are fixed until the economy reaches that point of equilibrium.[^24]</span>
9. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Investing a higher percentage will help but eventually we will still reach the steady-state, only later.[^24]</span>
#### Breaking the Steady-State
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">New ideas, inventions, and technological developments are what allow countries to keep on producing more per capita.[^25]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">With new technology, people can produce more with the same input of resources.[^25]</span>
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">As such, as long as new technology keeps on being developed, an economy does not have to enter the steady-state. It can keep growing. This is because with new technology, the same capital can produce more, so the rate of production can "stay ahead" of the rate of [[#Depreciation|depreciation]], despite the [[#Diminishing Returns|diminishing returns]].[^25]</span>
4. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This is why  GDP per capita in large and developed countries continues to grow.[^25]</span>
5. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Ideas and inventions are cultivated by the [[#Factors that Lead to High GDP|factors that lead to high GDP]].[^26]</span>
   <span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> In the United States, there are online platforms to sell ideas and to find investors for a start-up. There are laws protecting intellectual property (patents). And being an industry leader is actually something that is admired and looked up to in society. You can be a celebrity for being an industry leader (like Steve Jobs and Elon Musk, for example). In addition, one keeps the profits for an invention that they create. 70% of inventions in the United States are for-profit.[^26]
### Undeveloped vs Developed Countries
1. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">According to the Solow Model, undeveloped countries should have a faster increase in GDP per capita than developed countries.[^23]</span>
2. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">This is because developed countries suffer from a fair amount of [[#Diminishing Returns|diminishing returns]], meaning, the more a country develops, the less of a difference an increase in physical capital or educated labor make.</span>
   Conversely, the less a country has developed, the greater of a difference an increase in physical capital or educated labor make.[^23]
3. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">However, this is only true for countries that have similar [[#Reasons for Such Development|factors that foster growth]].[^23]</span>
4. <span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">In addition, this assumes that the undeveloped countries can use the technologies that have been invented in the developed countries.[^18]</span>
