# Bad Methods
## Sample of Convenience
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Use some convenient way of choosing the participants in the sample. This can introduce bias because there might be a reason why some people are conveniently found.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;"><span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A school is trying to find out about how many (or few) students like their classes. They decide that someone should walk around the hallways and ask the first 50 students about their classes. This can introduce bias because there might be a reason why these kids are in the hall. Maybe these are the kids who are usually late to class. Or maybe these kids have already finished their homework so they can hang out in the hallways and the kids who need to finish their homework are in the classrooms. And there could be many more reasons why this particular sample may have a particular bias.</span>
## Volunteer Response Sampling
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Have people volunteer to be sampled. This can introduce bias because there may be a specific type of person who would like to volunteer to be sampled about something.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;"><span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A school is trying to find out about how many (or few) students like their classes. It decides to tell students that they can go onto the school's website and fill out an online survey about how much they enjoy their classes. This can introduce bias because perhaps only the kids with strong opinions will be the ones to go fill this out. Or perhaps only the kids who have a lot of time on their hands will go fill it out. Maybe only the goody-goods will go and listen to what the school says. (As a side note, this introduces another kind of bias. Only kids with internet access can answer the survey.)</span>
# Good Methods
## Simple Random Sample
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">Everyone in a population has an even chance of being selected, and some type of random selector is used.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;"><span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A school is trying to find out about how many (or few) students like their classes. It decides to have a random number generator pick some number corresponding with the students' ID numbers (assuming everyone has a number and the random number generator can pick any of them).</span>
## Stratified Random Sample
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The population is split into groups and members of these groups are chosen at random. This method has a particular example in that it can help guarantee that there is proper representation from all of the groups. This is the same as [[Randomly Dividing Control Group and Treatment Group#Block Design|block design]]. When this strategy is used for selecting samples it is called stratified random sampling, and when it is done for experiments, it is called block design.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;"><span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A school is trying to find out about how many (or few) students like their classes. It decided to divide the students by grade level and then pick a sample from each grade level. By using a stratified method, the surveyors can be sure that their sample contains a representative number of students from each grade.</span>
## Cluster Random Sample
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The population is randomly split into clusters. Then, clusters are randomly selected and every member of the cluster is part of the sample.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;"><span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> An airline wants to find out about how good its flights are, so it selects a few flights at random and surveys everyone on these flights.</span>
## Systematic Random Sample
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;">The population is put into some random order (or they put themselves into a some order). A starting point is randomly selected, and then from there on, every $x$ number person (like every 20<sup>th</sup> person, for example), is chosen.</span>
<span style="display: block; text-align: justify;"><span style="color:rgb(112, 48, 160)">Example:</span> A school is trying to find out about how many (or few) students like their classes. It decided that it will survey the 30<sup>th</sup> student that comes that morning, and then every 30<sup>th</sup> student after that.</span>
